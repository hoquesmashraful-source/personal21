# Introduction, gap and objective

## What the evidence shows

From the full introductions of 18 highly ranked empirical top papers:

* Short: median 3 paragraphs (range 2 to 6) and 452 words. In the open
  access comparison, top introductions had a median of 492 words versus 435
  for controls.
* All 18 state the aim or contribution in the final paragraph.
* None states a formal hypothesis; aims are framed as estimation targets.
  Analytic papers name the exposure and outcome in the aim.
* 6 of 18 use "to our knowledge" once. 4 include a Lancet "Research in
  context" panel with the search behind the gap claim.

## Paragraph moves

1. **Burden and stakes.** Why the topic matters, usually with one number or
   a policy target.
2. **What is known.** Prior estimates named with their place, years and
   scope, credited, then their limits.
3. **Gap and aim.** The missing piece, why it matters now, then "In this
   study, we...".

**Ways to frame the burden:** a definition then the stake then a cost; a
prior estimate of deaths; a threat with a disputed forecast, flagged as
disputed; a policy target (for example a 2030 goal); a progress contrast
(disability falling far more slowly than early death).

## How the gap is stated

The strongest gaps follow one chain: **what is known, what is missing, why it
matters now.** Earlier estimates are listed by agency, region and year,
credited as important, then shown to be insufficient. A short pivot follows
("To address these gaps...").

| Gap type | Example PMIDs |
| --- | --- |
| Coverage: places, ages or causes missing | 15364185, 31954465, 35065702, 29029897 |
| Out of date | 17132052, 33069326 |
| Measurement or method problem | 23245607, 26050253 |
| Comparability or synthesis needed | 23245609, 29253477 |
| Contested or inconclusive evidence | 11948273, 11879110 |
| Neglected problem | 16980116, 32061315 |
| New event | 31986261, 34634250 |

The sharpest single gap is a **mismatch between burden and evidence**: most
cardiovascular burden falls in poorer countries, yet most risk evidence came
from richer ones (INTERHEART, PMID 15364185).

**"Why now"** is tied to a dated trigger: a UN meeting, a new law or
guideline, a pandemic, a new data source.

## How the objective is stated

* Final paragraph, first person: "In this study, we present...", "We aimed
  to...". JAMA objectives start "To estimate...".
* Verbs: estimate, quantify, present, report, describe, compare, evaluate.
* The aim names the measure, population, places and period, often "by age
  and sex".
* Multiple aims are numbered ("We had three aims: to..., to... and to...").

## Signalling novelty without overclaiming

* Scope the word "first" ("the first estimates that cover all ages and
  include uncertainty").
* Use "to our knowledge" once, and only after a real search.
* Credit earlier work before naming its limits.
* Say how the new results update earlier ones.
* Admit the main limit of your own method early.
* Avoid "novel" as praise.

## Sentence frames

1. Coverage gap: "Earlier studies estimated [quantity] only for [restricted
   places, ages or years]. None covered [missing scope] with one consistent
   method."
2. Out of date: "The latest estimates of [quantity] use data from [year].
   Since then, [new data, exposure change or policy] has made them
   unreliable."
3. Measurement: "Most estimates of [indicator] rely on [weaker measure].
   This likely [understates or overstates] [quantity], especially in
   [group]."
4. Burden and evidence mismatch: "Most of the burden of [condition] falls in
   [setting A]. Yet most evidence on [factor] comes from [setting B]."
5. Why now: "[Named target or law] requires [specific estimate] by [year].
   Without it, [decision makers] cannot [specific decision]."
6. Objective: "In this study, we [estimate, quantify or compare] [measure] of
   [outcome] for [population] in [N places] from [year] to [year]."
7. Several aims: "We had three aims: to [aim 1], to [aim 2] and to [aim 3]."
8. Scoped novelty: "To our knowledge, these are the first estimates of
   [quantity] for [population] that [cover all ages, include uncertainty or
   use measured data]."

## Weaknesses to avoid

* A gap that stays inside one site with no link to a population decision.
* Burden presented as the reason for the study, with no gap.
* "Paucity of literature" without saying what is missing.
* Long chains of statistics from global to national.
* Soft aims ("shed light on").
* Unscoped "first" claims.

## Checklist

* [ ] Three or four paragraphs, about 400 to 500 words (or journal limit)
* [ ] Prior work named with place, years and scope, and credited
* [ ] Gap: what is known, what is missing, why it matters now
* [ ] A dated "why now" trigger, if there is one
* [ ] Final paragraph: aim with verb, measure, population, places, period
* [ ] Any "first" claim scoped and checked
