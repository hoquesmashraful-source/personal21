## 1. The evidence base

* **Top 100:** the most cited public health articles published 2000 to 2024
  (NIH iCite counts, October 2026). Sources: 22 core public health and
  epidemiology journals, plus public-health-indexed articles in the Lancet,
  BMJ, JAMA, NEJM, PLOS Medicine and Nature Medicine. 82 papers about
  individual clinical care were screened out. Citations ranged from 3,122 to
  15,076 (median 4,496).
* **Controls:** 163 articles with an abstract, drawn at random from the same
  journal and year as each top paper (median 171 citations). Matching rules
  out journal and year as explanations; it does not rule out topic, type or
  team.
* **Manual coding** of all 100 by contribution type and by what citing
  authors most likely reuse (single coder; see `top100-classification.md`).

## 2. The central finding: papers are cited as infrastructure

Most top papers are cited because other papers need them, not because they
announced a surprising result.

| What citing authors reuse | Top papers |
| --- | --- |
| A number for the Introduction (burden, prevalence, trend) | 70 |
| A method or data source for the Methods | 12 |
| A finding to compare against in the Discussion | 9 |
| A framework or concept | 6 |
| A standard or cut-off used in measurement | 3 |

| Contribution type | Top papers | Median citations |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Global, national or multi-country estimates | 56 (23 from the Global Burden of Disease series) | 4,743 |
| Synthesis (review, meta-analysis, Lancet Series or Commission) | 12 | 4,266 |
| Landmark finding | 10 | 3,740 |
| Method | 7 | 3,897 |
| Framework or concept | 5 | 3,930 |
| Data resource or cohort profile | 4 | 3,769 |
| Standard or reference | 3 | 6,035 |
| First data on an outbreak | 3 | 8,865 |

Landmark findings are rare and large: 9 of the 10 used national data or more
than 20,000 people. By the rule-based count, single-setting original research
made up 25% of top papers versus 64% of controls.

## 3. Ten citation drivers, with evidence

Rates are top 100 versus controls.

**1. A reusable output.** The abstract offers estimates, a tool, a framework
or data in 41% versus 21%. Supplementary material: 38% versus 21%. Code or
data shared: 42% versus 28% (open access full texts). The modified Poisson
paper (PMID 15033648) has one author and a five-sentence abstract; it solves
a routine problem every analyst meets. Methods earn citations from every
user: an applied paper using marginal structural models has 77 citations;
the paper introducing them (PMID 10955408) has 3,897.

**2. Scale and comprehensiveness.** Title names global, world or many
countries: 43% versus 12%. Abstract names 10 or more countries: 25% versus
10%. Sample of 100,000 or more: 49% versus 20%. Comprehensiveness beats
brand: the GBD 2019 capstone (PMID 33069326) has 15,076 citations; a
same-issue GBD overview has 478. A complete, consistent list becomes the
default reference.

**3. Filling a citation slot every paper needs.** An opening burden figure
(NHANES obesity series, GBD), a method justification (the events-per-variable
paper, PMID 17182981; MR-Egger, PMID 26050253), a measurement standard (BMI
cut-offs for Asian populations, PMID 14726171; WHO growth reference, PMID
18026621) or a data source (CHARLS, MESA, UK Biobank, Swedish registers). A
slot is only as large as its user community: CHARLS (open, harmonised,
national) has 4,184 citations; a local surveillance-site profile in the same
journal and year has 29.

**4. Timeliness with data.** Five COVID-19 papers had relative citation ratios
of 299 to 707 (top-100 median 182). They gave reusable parameters (for
example an incubation period and reproduction number, PMID 31995857).
COVID-19 commentary without data in the same journals and years drew 3 to 39
citations.

**5. Series and updates.** 23 GBD papers over seven rounds; five NHANES
obesity updates (3,931 to 6,196 citations each); NCD-RisC; the Lancet
dementia Commission. Each round replaces the last and reopens the slot.
Series membership alone is not enough.

**6. Quantified, uncertainty-bounded headline numbers.** Numbers per 100
abstract words: 9.5 versus 6.3. Interval in the abstract: 48% versus 37%.
Uncertainty intervals or bootstrap in the full text: 70% versus 20%. The best
headline numbers can be lifted in one sentence: "4.95 million deaths
associated with bacterial AMR in 2019 (3.62 to 6.57 million)" (PMID
35065702); "14.9 million preterm births in 2010, 11.1% of livebirths" (PMID
22682464).

**7. Clear, informative titles and self-contained abstracts.** Colon: 65%
versus 48%. Time span in the title: 30% versus 7%. Question titles: 1% versus
3%. Structured abstracts: 77% versus 60%. Fewer hedges (1.8 versus 3.1 per
1,000 words) and fewer passives (8.3 versus 11.4). A title can itself be a
citable phrase ("Actual causes of death in the United States, 2000").

**8. Collaboration and consortia.** Group author: 35% versus 17%. Any grant
listed: 69% versus 51%. Yet 65% of top papers had no group author, and
several had one to three authors. Consortia buy coverage; coverage drives
scale.

**9. Journal and visibility.** 76 of 100 are in the Lancet, JAMA or NEJM, yet
same-journal controls have a median of 171 citations. The journal is a gate
and an amplifier, not a guarantee. Field journals reached the list through
reusable products: 13 of 14 such papers were methods, standards, frameworks
or data resources.

**10. Open access.** 32% versus 17%, but mostly a GBD effect (15 of 23 GBD
papers are open access, against 17 of the other 77). Open access may follow
fame rather than cause it. Treat it as a modest helper.

## 4. What an individual author cannot copy

* Journal prestige: editors decide.
* Consortium scale and long-term funding.
* The Matthew effect: once a paper is the default reference, authors and
  reviewers expect it.
* Rare global events (COVID-19 timing).
* Self-citation within large series (not measurable here).
* Topic size: 15 of 100 titles name obesity, overweight or BMI; large fields
  produce more citing papers.

## 5. Correlation, not cause

Controls match on journal and year only; topic, type, team and funding still
differ. GBD features (intervals, maps, consortia, open access, one funder)
travel together, so no single one can be isolated. Open access, supplements
and updates may follow success. We see the winners. Present these as
patterns worth considering, never as rules that guarantee citations.

## 6. Citability guide: design choices

1. **Decide the reuse first.** Finish the sentence "Future authors will cite
   this for ___" before designing. Every top paper fills a slot.
2. **Size the question to the largest honest unit.** National data, all
   causes, all ages, pooled studies, many countries, where the data allow.
3. **Produce one quotable, uncertainty-bounded number** with its definition,
   population, year and interval.
4. **Turn a method into a named, simple tool** with a worked example and
   shared code.
5. **Name and define a construct** when the field lacks one, with a short
   name and a precise definition.
6. **Publish a profile or validation** of any dataset you curate, with clear
   access rules.
7. **Be first with data, not opinion**, on an emerging threat, and report
   parameters others can reuse.
8. **Build for updates**: stable methods and indicators so each update
   replaces the last.
9. **Pool when pooling adds coverage**; collaborate to reach scale.
10. **Ship reusable tables, maps and trend figures**, and downloadable
    estimates.

## 7. Citability guide: writing choices

11. **Title:** topic, population, scale and period; design after a colon; no
    question.
12. **Abstract:** structured, self-contained, headline number with interval
    first in the Findings; key terms defined.
13. **Direct language:** hedge once at the point of doubt; discuss
    limitations openly in the Discussion (79% versus 62%).
14. **Access:** deposit the accepted manuscript where allowed; share code and
    estimates.

## 8. What not to do

* Do not chase citations: no coercive or reciprocal self-citation, no padded
  reference lists.
* Do not overstate causality to sound quotable. Use "attributable" only with
  a design and counterfactual that support it.
* Do not inflate scope words: a "global" title must match the data.
* Do not publish a bare number without definition, denominator, year and
  interval.
* Do not slice one dataset into thin papers.
* Do not rush data-free commentary on a hot topic.
* Do not treat citations as the goal. Citations measure reuse, not truth or
  health impact.
