# Discussion

This study is the first study to examine early childhood development across eight countries in sub-Saharan Africa using MICS6 data and we found that only 61.3% (95% CI 59.8 to 62.7) of children aged 36–59 months were developmentally on track, which is a very worrying finding that shows the crisis in early childhood development in the region. Furthermore, children whose mothers had secondary or higher education were more likely to be on track (aOR 1.84, 95% CI 1.52 to 2.23), which shows that maternal education causes better development.

Our findings are consistent with previous studies [12,13]. Moreover, attending early childhood education programmes had a strong impact on development (aOR 2.10, 95% CI 1.71 to 2.58) — this means that governments should immediately expand early childhood education to all children. Additionally, children exposed to violent discipline were less likely to be on track (aOR 0.81, 95% CI 0.70 to 0.94), and this proves that violent discipline harms children's development [14].

Interestingly, we found no association between household wealth and development after adjustment (aOR for richest vs poorest 1.09, 95% CI 0.88 to 1.35), which is different from other studies [15,16] but may be because wealth works through education and early learning.

This study has some limitations. First, it is cross-sectional. Second, ECDI is reported by mothers. Third, some variables were not available. Despite these limitations, the study has strengths such as large sample size and nationally representative data.

# Conclusion

In conclusion, only 61.3% of children were on track, and maternal education (aOR 1.84) and early childhood education (aOR 2.10) were the main determinants of child development, so policymakers must invest in maternal education and early childhood education programmes to improve child development in sub-Saharan Africa.
