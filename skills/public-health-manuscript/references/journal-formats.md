# Journal formats for high-impact general and global health journals

Formats change. Treat this as a starting point and check the journal's current
instructions for authors before submission, especially word limits.

| Journal | Abstract headings | Extra required element |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The Lancet, Lancet Global Health and other Lancet journals | Background; Methods; Findings; Interpretation; Funding | "Research in context" panel: Evidence before this study (with search strategy), Added value of this study, Implications of all the available evidence |
| BMJ | Objective; Design; Setting; Participants; Main outcome measures; Results; Conclusions | "What is already known on this topic" and "What this study adds" box; patient and public involvement statement |
| BMJ Global Health | Introduction (or Background); Methods; Results; Conclusions | Summary box: what is already known, what this study adds, how it might affect research, practice or policy |
| BMJ Open | Objectives; Design; Setting; Participants; Outcome measures; Results; Conclusions | "Strengths and limitations of this study": short bullet points after the abstract, about the methods only |
| JAMA, JAMA Network Open | Importance; Objective; Design, Setting, and Participants; Exposure(s); Main Outcomes and Measures; Results; Conclusions and Relevance | Key Points: Question, Findings, Meaning (about 100 words) |
| PLOS Medicine | Background; Methods and findings; Conclusions | Author summary: Why was this study done? What did the researchers do and find? What do these findings mean? Limitations must appear in the abstract's Methods and findings |
| NEJM | Background; Methods; Results; Conclusions | Short abstract (about 250 words) |
| Nature Medicine | Unstructured single paragraph (about 150 words) | Methods placed after the main text; detailed reporting summary |
| Bulletin of the WHO | Objective; Methods; Findings; Conclusion | Short, policy-facing conclusion |

## What differs between these journals and typical field journals

Findings from the corpus of highly cited DHS and MICS papers:

* **Results heading in the abstract.** Lancet journals use "Findings" and
  "Interpretation"; PLOS Medicine merges "Methods and findings". Use the
  journal's exact headings.
* **Context panels.** Lancet's "Research in context" asks for a described
  literature search (databases, dates, terms) before the study. Draft it from
  the Introduction's evidence gap and the Discussion's comparison paragraph.
* **Funding and role of the funder.** Lancet abstracts end with Funding.
  All of these journals require a statement on the funder's role.
* **Numbers style.** The Lancet uses a raised decimal point (1·45) and
  thin-space thousands. Most others use a full stop. In drafts write
  "1.45 (95% CI 1.20 to 1.75)" and let the journal apply house style.
* **Limitations.** PLOS Medicine and BMJ Open want limitations near the front
  (abstract or bullet points). Lancet and BMJ want an honest limitations
  paragraph in the Discussion.
* **Data sharing.** All of these journals expect a data availability
  statement. For DHS and MICS, say the data are publicly available on
  registration and give the source.

## Choosing the effect measure for a common outcome

Top-journal reviewers often question odds ratios when the outcome is common
(prevalence above about 10%), because odds ratios then overstate risk ratios.
Options: report adjusted prevalence ratios from modified Poisson or
log-binomial models, or report predicted probabilities or marginal effects
alongside odds ratios. Say which you used and why.
