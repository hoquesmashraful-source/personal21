# Discussion

Limitations and strengths are in `limitations.md`; the Conclusion is in
`conclusion.md`.

## What the corpus shows

* Median 1,155 words (IQR 897 to 1,488), about 8 paragraphs.
* Content: comparison with other studies 88%, policy implications 80%,
  limitations 73%, mechanisms 58%, generalisability 52%, strengths 46%,
  future research 28%.
* Opening: 34% restate the main finding first. In a close reading of 40
  exemplars, about three quarters of top-journal papers stated the main
  finding within two sentences.
* Hedging rate (may, might, could, suggest, likely) was similar across
  journal tiers, about 10 per 1,000 words. Top-journal papers attach hedges
  to specific explanations rather than spreading them over every sentence.
* About 12 of 20 top-journal papers stated the likely direction of at least
  one bias, against 5 of 20 others. Top papers also checked their estimates
  against other evidence (trials, validation studies, other data sources).

## Paragraph moves, in order

1. **Opening summary (one paragraph).** Answer the research question in
   words, with at most one or two headline numbers. Do not restate the aim
   or background. If there is a key caveat, build it into this paragraph.
2. **Comparison with other studies (one to three paragraphs).** Where you
   agree and where you differ, then why: data, definitions, adjustment,
   period, setting. Compare with the strongest evidence (trials, cohorts,
   systematic reviews), not only similar surveys.
3. **Explanations and mechanisms (one to three paragraphs).** Named, cited
   pathways, hedged to match the evidence. The best papers use their own
   data to choose between explanations (for example, comparing maternal and
   paternal age gradients to separate biological from social pathways).
4. **Unexpected, null or heterogeneous findings.** Name it, check for
   artefact first (measurement, missing data, survey differences), then
   offer competing explanations and a way to test them.
5. **Implications for policy and programmes (one or two paragraphs).** Tie
   each implication to a specific finding, a population and, where possible,
   a named actor. Measurement implications (new survey questions, better
   indicators) are often the safest and most useful.
6. **Strengths and limitations** (see `limitations.md`), usually near the
   end; BMJ-style papers may place them after the principal findings.
7. **Future research.** Name the design that would answer the open
   question (cohort, trial, qualitative study, linked facility data).

Lancet-family papers often end the Discussion with an implications
paragraph instead of a separate Conclusion. BMJ-family papers often use
subheadings: principal findings, comparison with other studies, strengths
and limitations, implications. JAMA papers use a Limitations subheading and
a short Conclusions section.

## Building each paragraph

Claim, evidence, interpretation, link:

* Claim: one sentence stating the finding in words.
* Evidence: one or two sentences citing studies that agree or disagree.
* Interpretation: a hedged explanation, with any caveat it needs.
* Link: why it matters for the question, policy or the next paragraph.

Avoid the "catalogue" Discussion, where each covariate gets its own
paragraph. Group findings by theme or by research question.

## Language

**Association versus causation**
* Use "was associated with", "higher odds of", "linked to". Avoid "effect",
  "impact", "determines", "led to", "increased the risk" for cross-sectional
  estimates.
* Name reverse causation where plausible (for example, illness reducing
  household wealth) and flag mediators that were adjusted for.
* Say plainly what the data can and cannot show; survey data are often
  stronger for surveillance and targeting than for explaining causes.

**Hedging**
* "Likely" for well supported claims; "may" or "might" for plausible ones.
* Mark speculation openly ("we speculate").
* Do not stack hedges ("may possibly suggest"), and do not cancel a careful
  hedge later with "clearly demonstrates".

**Null and surprising results**
* "We found no clear evidence of [association] ([estimate, 95% CI])", and
  say what size of effect the interval cannot rule out.
* Never flag a surprise and leave it unexplained.

## Sentence frames

Use `[ref]` placeholders for citations the user has not supplied. Never
invent references.

1. "Across [N] [DHS or MICS] surveys in [setting, years], [exposure] was
   associated with [higher or lower] [outcome], and this pattern was
   [consistent or variable] across [countries or subgroups]."
2. "Our findings suggest that [main answer], although [measure or sample]
   captures only [aspect]."
3. "This agrees with [study type] from [setting] [ref], which also found
   [finding]."
4. "Our estimate is [smaller or larger] than that of [ref], probably
   because we [used individual data, adjusted for X, studied a later
   period]."
5. "One plausible pathway is [mechanism]. If it operates, we would expect
   [testable pattern], which we [observed or could not test]."
6. "The association may also run the other way, because [outcome] could
   reduce [exposure] through [route]."
7. "Contrary to our expectation, [finding]. This may partly reflect
   [artefact], so it needs confirmation in [design or setting]."
8. "We found no clear evidence of [association] ([estimate, 95% CI]), but
   the interval does not rule out a [size] difference."
9. "If confirmed by longitudinal or intervention studies, these findings
   would support [action] for [group] in [setting]."
10. "Because [group or area] had the lowest [coverage], [programme] could
    prioritise it when allocating [resource]."

## Weaknesses to avoid

* Restating results paragraph by paragraph with odds ratios and p values.
* New results in the Discussion ("data not shown"). Move them to Results or
  the supplement.
* Methods tutorials in the Discussion.
* Causal verbs or causal recommendations from cross-sectional associations.
* Explanations drawn from general context rather than linked to the data.
* Surprises flagged but not explained.
* Unchecked claims that findings "can be generalised" to a whole region.
* Novelty claims ("this is the first study") unless the user has verified
  them with a stated search; prefer describing what the study adds.

## Checklist

* [ ] First paragraph answers the question in words, with at most two numbers
* [ ] Each main finding compared with named studies; differences explained
* [ ] Mechanisms cited, hedged and, where possible, tested with own data
* [ ] Reverse causation and mediation named where plausible
* [ ] Nulls reported with intervals; surprises checked for artefact
* [ ] Each implication names a group, an action and the finding it rests on
* [ ] No causal verbs for cross-sectional associations
* [ ] No new results or methods detail
* [ ] Length suits the journal (corpus: 900 to 1,500 words, 6 to 10 paragraphs)
