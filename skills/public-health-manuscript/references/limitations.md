# Limitations and strengths

## What the corpus shows

* 73% of Discussions state limitations; 30% give them their own heading or
  subheading. 55% place them in the last three paragraphs.
* Common topics: self-report or recall bias (36%), cross-sectional design and
  causality (35%), unmeasured confounding (18%).
* About 12 of 20 top-journal exemplars stated the likely direction of at
  least one bias, against 5 of 20 others. The weak form is a short list with
  no consequence ("First, it is cross-sectional. Second, data are
  self-reported.").

## The four-part limitation

Write each important limitation in four parts:

1. **The problem.** What is imperfect, specifically.
2. **How it could bias results.** The mechanism.
3. **Likely direction and size.** Towards or away from the null; over- or
   underestimate; small or large, with evidence if available.
4. **What was done about it.** A sensitivity analysis, a restriction, a
   validation study cited, or why it is unlikely to change the conclusion.

Order limitations by importance, not by convention. Two or three well argued
limitations persuade more than a list of eight.

## Limitations specific to DHS and MICS

| Issue | What to say |
| --- | --- |
| Cross-sectional design | Exposure and outcome measured together. Name the specific reverse pathway (for example, illness lowering wealth). Covariates are measured at interview, not at the time of the birth or event studied. |
| Self-report and recall | Recall of care up to five years earlier is imperfect; cite validation studies; restricting to the most recent birth reduces recall error. Over-reporting usually inflates coverage. DHS and MICS use different recall periods. |
| Social desirability and under-disclosure | Sensitive behaviours (violence, sexual behaviour, abortion) are under-reported, so prevalence is a lower bound; under-reporting may vary by setting. |
| Survivor and live-birth bias | Only live births and surviving children are recorded; stillbirths and children who died lack some care variables. Usually biases estimates in a stated direction; say which. |
| Missing confounders | Name the confounder (for example, obstetric risk, care quality) and the likely direction of bias. Mention proxies used. |
| GPS displacement | Cluster coordinates are displaced (up to 2 km urban, 5 km rural), adding random error that tends to understate distance effects. Linked area data can cause regression dilution and ecological fallacy. |
| Comparability across countries and rounds | Questionnaire items, urban definitions and survey years differ. State the spread of survey years. |
| Sample restriction | Who is excluded (unmarried women, women without recent births, men) and what that means for generalisability. Compare included and excluded groups where possible. |
| Wealth measurement | Relative, survey-specific index; asset choice changes who is "poor"; may include water and sanitation; slow to change; urban bias. Residual confounding by socioeconomic position is likely. |
| Multiple comparisons | Many covariates and subgroups raise the chance of false positive findings. |

## Strengths without boasting

Name a design feature and say which bias it reduces. Nationally
representative sampling supports generalisation to the survey population;
standardised questionnaires support comparison across countries; a large
sample gives precise estimates. Pair each strength with its limit. Avoid
generic lines ("large sample size and high response rate") that are not
linked to validity.

## Sentence frames

1. "[Variable] was self-reported and may be [over or under]-reported by
   [group]. If so, the association with [outcome] is likely
   [overstated or understated]."
2. "Only [live births or surviving children] were recorded. Because the
   excluded [group] were probably [more or less] exposed, our estimate is
   likely biased towards [the null or away from the null]."
3. "Because exposure and outcome were measured at the same time, we cannot
   establish their temporal order; [outcome] may also reduce [exposure]
   through [route]."
4. "We could not adjust for [confounder], which is associated with both
   [exposure] and [outcome]. This would probably [inflate or attenuate] the
   association."
5. "To test this, we [sensitivity analysis]. The estimate changed from
   [value] to [value], so [the conclusion holds or needs caution]."
6. "Cluster coordinates are displaced to protect confidentiality, which adds
   random error to [distance] and probably understates its association
   with [outcome]."
7. "Our use of nationally representative surveys with standardised
   questionnaires supports comparison across [countries], although survey
   years ranged from [year] to [year]."

## Checklist

* [ ] Each important limitation: problem, bias mechanism, direction, mitigation
* [ ] DHS or MICS issues covered where relevant (table above)
* [ ] Sensitivity analyses or validation studies tied to specific limitations
* [ ] Strengths specific, linked to validity, paired with their limits
* [ ] No limitation contradicts a claim made elsewhere in the paper
