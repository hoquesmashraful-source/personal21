# Title, abstract and front-matter boxes

Abstract headings by journal are in `journal-formats.md`.

## Title

### What the corpus shows
* Median 16 words. 66% use a colon; 89% name the country or region; 32%
  name the data source; 32% use "factors associated" or "determinants".
* In 40 exemplars, top-journal titles differed sharply from the rest:
  * 9 of 20 stated scale (numbers of countries or surveys), against 2 of 20.
  * None named DHS or MICS; they wrote "national household surveys". Seven
    of the 20 others named the survey.
  * 18 of 20 were multi-country; none was a question.
  * Only 1 used "factors associated" or "determinants".

### Moves
1. Topic: outcome, or exposure and outcome (5 to 10 words).
2. Population and setting.
3. Colon.
4. Design, data and scale ("an analysis of 91 national household surveys";
   "a cross-sectional study"; "a multilevel analysis").
5. Optional period.

### Frames
1. "[Outcome] and its inequalities across [N] low-income and middle-income
   countries: an analysis of [N] national household surveys"
2. "[Exposure] and [outcome] among [population] in [region]: a multi-country
   cross-sectional study"
3. "Trends in [indicator] by [wealth or residence] in [N] countries, [year]
   to [year]: a cross-sectional study"
4. "[Short hook]: [what was measured] in [setting]"
5. "Factors associated with [outcome] in [country]: a multilevel analysis of
   the [year] Demographic and Health Survey" (fits field journals better
   than general journals)

### Avoid
Causal words (impact, effect) for associations; abbreviations; vague labels
("a demographic study"); more than about 20 words.

## Abstract

### What the corpus shows
* 85% structured. Median 313 words: Background about 58, Methods 73,
  Results 124, Conclusion 54.
* 82% name survey years, 78% give percentages, 43% give 95% CIs, 39% give
  the sample size, 28% give an effect estimate, 12% give p values.
* Top-journal exemplars carried more numbers. 11 of 20 gave interval
  estimates, against 4 of 20 others. 8 of 20 named the countries at each end
  of a range, against 1 of 20. 16 of 20 used "we" in Methods, against 10.
* Every Lancet-family exemplar ended with Funding; PLOS Medicine abstracts
  from 2016 included a limitation sentence.

### Moves
1. **Context** (one to three sentences): burden or policy target.
2. **Gap** (optional, one sentence): what is not known.
3. **Aim** (one sentence).
4. **Methods** (three to five sentences): survey, years, countries,
   population and n, outcome definition, model and effect measure, design
   adjustment.
5. **Results or Findings** (four to seven sentences): open with one headline
   estimate and its 95% CI; then the main contrast with an adjusted estimate
   and CI; then the range with the extremes named; then secondary results.
6. **Limitation** (one sentence, where the journal asks for it).
7. **Conclusion or Interpretation** (two or three sentences): the finding in
   words, then one concrete action, monitoring change or research need that
   the design supports.
8. **Funding** (Lancet journals).

### Frames
1. "[Indicator] remains [high or unequal] in [setting], but [specific
   unknown] has not been measured."
2. "We aimed to [estimate, compare or test] [what] across [N] countries."
3. "We pooled [N] [DHS or MICS] surveys from [N] countries ([year] to
   [year]), covering [n] [unit]."
4. "We defined [outcome] as [definition] and estimated [adjusted prevalence
   ratios] with [model], accounting for the survey design."
5. "Overall, [x]% (95% CI [a] to [b]) of [population] [had outcome], from
   [x]% in [country] to [y]% in [country]."
6. "Compared with [reference group], [group] had [higher or lower] odds of
   [outcome] (aOR [x], 95% CI [a] to [b])."
7. "The main limitation is [limitation], which may have [overstated or
   understated] [association]."
8. "[Finding in plain words]. [Monitoring systems or programmes] should
   [specific action] for [group]."

### Avoid
* A Background that never says what the study does.
* Software versions and data access notes in the abstract.
* Results with no numbers, unlabelled ranges, ratios without CIs, p values
  alone.
* Conclusions that outrun the data, including causal verbs ("reduced the
  probability") for cross-sectional findings.
* Numbers that differ from the main text.

## Front-matter boxes

Write these from the finished paper; they must not add claims.

**Research in context (Lancet journals)**
* Evidence before this study: the search (databases, dates, terms, language
  limits) and what it found, including its gaps. Use placeholders for search
  details the user has not given.
* Added value of this study: what this study adds, in two to four
  sentences.
* Implications of all the available evidence: what the combined evidence
  means for policy, practice or research.

**Key Points (JAMA journals, about 100 words)**
* Question: one sentence.
* Findings: design, population, n, and the main result with its size.
* Meaning: one sentence on implications, within the evidence.

**What is already known / What this study adds (BMJ journals)**
* Two or three short bullet points each; BMJ Global Health adds "How this
  study might affect research, practice or policy".

**Strengths and limitations of this study (BMJ Open)**
* Up to five short bullets about the methods only, not the results.

## Checklist
* [ ] Headings match the journal
* [ ] Survey, years, countries and sample size stated
* [ ] First Results sentence gives the headline number with its CI
* [ ] Every ratio labelled (aOR, PR) with a 95% CI
* [ ] Conclusion names an action or monitoring change the design supports
* [ ] Numbers identical to the main text; within the word limit
