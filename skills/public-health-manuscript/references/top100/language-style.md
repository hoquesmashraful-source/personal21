# Language style

## What the evidence shows

| Measure (abstracts) | Top 100 | Controls |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mean sentence length (words) | 26.8 | 26.3 |
| Sentences of 30 or more words | 33.5% | 30.9% |
| "We" or "our" per 1,000 words | 6.7 | 6.1 |
| Passive constructions per 1,000 words | 8.3 | 11.4 |

| Numbers per 1,000 words | 112.5 | 87.2 |

In Discussions: hedges 6.8 versus 9.8 per 1,000 words; causal verbs 4.6
versus 1.6; numbers 48.8 versus 39.4; mean sentence length 33 words in both.

**What separates top papers is not sentence length.** It is density of
numbers, fewer passives, fewer and better placed hedges, and confident
statements where the design earns them.

## Rules drawn from the top papers

* **Voice.** Use "we" for what you did ("We estimated"). Keep the passive
  for routine data steps ("Input data were extracted").
* **Tense.** Past for methods and your own results; present for settled
  facts and the general claim at the end; present perfect for trends ("has
  become").
* **Hedge once, at the point of doubt.** "may be levelling off in women";
  "probably largely causal". Never stack hedges, and do not hedge measured
  results.
* **Confident claims where the design earns them.** Causal verbs appear when
  a counterfactual is defined (attributable burden, decomposition) or when
  citing causal evidence. Cohort and cross-sectional findings keep
  "associated with".
* **Plain vocabulary.** Define technical metrics in plain words at first use
  (for example, the Socio-demographic Index as income, schooling and
  fertility). Outcomes are plain nouns: deaths, people, children.
* **Contrast inside the sentence** ("Although", "Despite", "Yet"), not stock
  transitions; top papers almost never use "Furthermore" or "Moreover".
* **Numbers carry the argument.** Give the estimate rather than "high" or
  "substantial".

## Keeping the strengths in sentences under 30 words

A third of top abstract sentences run to 30 words or more, mostly in
Findings: ranked lists, nested intervals and stacked qualifiers. The user of
this skill writes sentences under 30 words. Keep the density and drop the
length:

1. One number family per sentence: a level or a change, not both.
2. Open with the anchor (year, place), then the estimate, then the interval.
3. For lists longer than three, state the count first, then name the items;
   move their intervals to a table.
4. Turn each "which" or "with" tail into a new sentence.
5. Keep intervals for headline numbers; send secondary intervals to tables.
6. Define an acronym in its own short sentence when the gloss is long.

**Example**
* Long: "[Outcome] rose from [x]% ([CI]) in [year 1] to [y]% ([CI]) in [year
  2], driven mainly by [factor], which affected [group] most."
* Short: "[Outcome] rose from [x]% ([CI]) in [year 1] to [y]% ([CI]) in
  [year 2]. [Factor] drove most of this rise. [Group] was most affected."

(Use "drove" only if the analysis decomposed the change or cites causal
evidence; otherwise write "[Factor] accounted for most of this rise" or
describe the association.)

## House style for this user

* Sentences under 30 words; plain, familiar words; active voice where
  clearer.
* No em dashes, en dashes or double hyphens. Ranges with "to".
* No "Furthermore", "Moreover" or "Additionally".
* Estimates as "1.45 (95% CI 1.20 to 1.75)"; exact p values; p<0.001.
