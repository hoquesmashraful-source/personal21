# Titles and abstracts

## What the evidence shows

Top 100 versus 163 matched controls with an abstract (same journal and year):

| Feature | Top 100 | Controls |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Title has a colon | 65% | 48% |
| Title names global, world or many countries | 43% | 12% |
| Title states scale (countries, studies, people) | 19% | 5% |
| Title states a time span or trend | 30% | 7% |
| Title is a question | 1% | 3% |
| Abstract words (median) | 322 | 257 |
| Structured abstract | 77% | 60% |
| Numbers per 100 abstract words (median) | 9.5 | 6.3 |
| Abstract reports a CI or uncertainty interval | 48% | 37% |
| Abstract mentions a sample of 100,000 or more | 49% | 20% |
| Abstract offers something reusable (estimates, tool, framework, data) | 41% | 21% |

A title formula does not create citations. One control used the Global
Burden of Disease title formula and earned 1338 citations: far above the
control median, yet below every top paper. The title signals the
contribution; the contribution earns the citations.

## Titles

**Patterns in top titles**

1. **Scope, scale and span, then design.** Measure, number of causes or
   countries, years; design label after the colon ("a systematic analysis",
   "a pooled analysis of [n] population-based studies").
2. **Scale in the subtitle.** "one million adults in 61 prospective studies"
   (PMID 12493255).
3. **National surveillance formula.** "Prevalence of [condition] in the
   United States, [years]" (JAMA; PMIDs 16595758, 24570244).
4. **Exposure and outcome as plain nouns**, no verb, setting named.
5. **Method titles name the problem solved** (weak instruments, invalid
   instruments, the events-per-variable rule).
6. **A named study acronym** in brackets (INTERHEART, NCS-R, CHARLS,
   CONCORD-3).
7. **A forward horizon**: projections to 2030, 2040 or 2050.

Top titles describe the contribution; they rarely state the result. Only one
is a question.

**Frames**
1. "[Measure] of [condition] in [N] countries, [start year] to [end year]: a
   [design label]"
2. "[Exposure] and [outcome] among [N] [population] in [setting]: a [design
   label]"
3. "Trends in [indicator] by [age, sex or wealth] in [place], [years], with
   projections to [year]"
4. "Correcting [named bias] in [study design]: [method] with [validation]"

## Abstracts

**Where the words go** (structured abstracts, median words and share):

| Move | Top | Controls |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Background | 53 (11%) | 54 (17%) |
| Methods | 81 (23%) | 61 (19%) |
| Findings or Results | 114 (38%) | 92 (32%) |
| Interpretation or Conclusions | 56 (15%) | 40 (13%) |

Top papers spend less on background and more on findings.

**Moves**
1. **Background** (two sentences): burden, then gap, often then the aim
   ("We aimed to...").
2. **Methods**: the data and their scale first, then the model, then how
   uncertainty was handled.
3. **Findings**: the first sentence carries a number in 90% of structured top
   abstracts. Four builds recur: a time anchor then the headline count ("In
   2010, there were..."); a scope word first ("Globally", "Worldwide",
   "Overall"); the denominator first (studies or people included); or a
   direct finding (a trend or comparison).
4. **Interpretation**: a present-tense claim, then a named action. Top
   conclusions more often name an action and less often hedge. Recurring
   forms: a general claim ("[X] is a leading cause of death"); a one-line
   verdict ("prevalent and costly"); "Despite [progress], [problem]
   remains"; a conditional projection ("If post-2000 trends continue...");
   a named actor with an action.
5. **Funding** (Lancet journals).

**Numbers.** Lead with estimates and intervals, not tests: p values appear in
few top abstracts. Name "95% UI" or "95% CI" at first use. Translate big
numbers with a plain anchor ("one in four deaths worldwide").

**Journal formats seen in the top papers**

| Journal | Headings | Habits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lancet family | Background, Methods, Findings, Interpretation, Funding | Longest abstracts (median about 420 words); Research in context panel |
| JAMA | Importance, Objective, Design, Setting, and Participants, Exposures, Main Outcomes and Measures, Results, Conclusions and Relevance | Objective as an infinitive ("To estimate...") |
| NEJM | Background, Methods, Results, Conclusions | Short background; often a one-sentence conclusion |
| Am J Epidemiol, Epidemiology | Unstructured paragraph | Shorter; methods-focused |
| PLOS Medicine | Background, Methods and findings, Conclusions | Limitations stated in the abstract |

**Frames**
1. Background: "[Condition] caused an estimated [number] [deaths] in [place]
   in [year]. Yet [specific quantity] remains unknown for [population]."
2. Aim: "We aimed to estimate [quantity] by [age, sex and place] in
   [population] from [year] to [year]."
3. First finding: "In [year], [number] ([95% CI or UI]) [people] in [place]
   had [outcome], about one in [N] [group]."
4. Trend: "From [year] to [year], [indicator] [rose or fell] from [x]% ([CI])
   to [y]% ([CI]) in [group]."
5. Interpretation: "[Condition] is now a leading cause of [outcome] in
   [place]. [Named actor] should [specific action] in [priority group]."
6. Scoped claim: "These findings are consistent with [mechanism]. However,
   [named limitation] means we cannot yet conclude [stronger claim]."

## Weaknesses to avoid

* Opaque titles: metaphor, opinion or question.
* Results that open with baseline characteristics instead of the main
  estimate.
* Tests without intervals.
* Claims that outrun the design (national rollout from a small uncontrolled
  study).
* "Further research is needed" as the whole message.
* Heavy background, thin findings.

## Checklist

* [ ] Title names population or place, scale and time span; design after a
      colon; no question or metaphor
* [ ] Background is two or three sentences: burden, gap, aim
* [ ] Methods open with the data source and its size
* [ ] Findings take about a third of the words or more
* [ ] First Results sentence gives the headline estimate with an interval
* [ ] Main numbers carry a CI or UI; p values secondary or absent
* [ ] At least one big number has a plain anchor
* [ ] Interpretation: a present-tense claim, then a named action the design
      supports
