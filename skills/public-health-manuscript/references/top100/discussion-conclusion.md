# Discussion and Conclusion

## What the evidence shows

From the top 100 versus matched controls, and a close reading of the
Discussions of the 20 highest ranked empirical top papers with full text:

* Discussions are long (median 2,357 words versus 1,277). The extra length
  goes to comparison, explanation and limitations, not to repeating Results.
* Limitations are discussed in 79% of top full texts versus 62% of controls.
  In complete top Discussions they take about a quarter of the words.
* Top Discussions hedge less (6.8 versus 9.8 hedges per 1,000 words), give
  more numbers (48.8 versus 39.4) and use slightly fewer passive
  constructions. They state measured results plainly and hedge explanations.
* Conclusions are short (median 150 words versus 192). No visible closing
  ended on a limitation.

## Moves, in order

1. **Headline findings** (about 130 words). The main result in the first
   paragraph, with a number and, ideally, its interval. Keep novelty claims
   modest and specific.
2. **Drivers and implications**, finding by finding, with the strength of
   evidence marked.
3. **Comparison with earlier estimates** and why they differ.
4. **Strengths**, short and concrete.
5. **Limitations**, specific, with direction and what was done.
6. **Take-home paragraph** with one claim and one call to action.

Long Discussions use subheadings ("Main findings", "Comparison with other
estimates", "Strengths and limitations", "Implications"). Some papers place
policy after the limitations so the last word is an implication.

## Comparing your numbers with earlier estimates

Ten of the 20 close-read papers set their number beside a named earlier
figure. The best go beyond "consistent with":

1. Name the source and both numbers, and state the gap.
2. Compare like with like (restrict to the units both studies cover).
3. Decompose the gap into its inputs.
4. Sort reasons by type: more data, changed definitions, different methods,
   different counterfactuals.
5. Check whether intervals overlap.
6. Distrust easy agreement: shared inputs make matching estimates less
   independent.
7. If studies are not comparable, say so, then find common ground.
8. Take a reasoned position when you think an earlier estimate was wrong, and
   give the reason.
9. Benchmark against familiar burdens ("more people have CKD than diabetes").

Two top papers disagreed in print about deaths from physical inactivity and
each explained the method behind its figure (PMIDs 22818936 and 23245609). A
clear, reasoned comparison is itself something others cite.

## Implications within the evidence

* Tie each implication to a specific finding and, where possible, a named
  actor (policy makers, programme managers, clinicians, data systems).
* Test targets against observed trends rather than just citing them (for
  example, judging whether a 2025 or SDG target is attainable at current
  rates of change).
* Tailor responses by setting when drivers differ.
* Treat data systems as policy: name the surveillance or registration
  improvement needed.
* Grade the verb: "should" when effectiveness is shown elsewhere, "could"
  when plausible, "needs evaluation" when untested.
* Say what the design cannot show ("our descriptive analysis does not
  measure the drivers").

## Limitations

* Be specific: name the data source, region or step.
* Give direction and, if possible, size ("likely conservative"; "single
  tests may overestimate prevalence by [x] to [y]%").
* Admit when the direction is unknown.
* Pair each limitation with what you did (a correction, a rerun without the
  weak data, a bound).
* Bound the problem where you can ("the true value probably lies between the
  two counterfactual estimates").
* Number them in long Discussions; end with the fix or next step.

## Language

* State measured results plainly; use one graded hedge per explanation
  ("almost certainly", "probably", "might", "it is unclear"). Never stack
  hedges.
* Earn strong statements with numbers.
* Causal verbs appear more in top Discussions (4.6 versus 1.6 per 1,000
  words), but mainly for decomposition ("driven by population ageing"),
  counterfactual burden and cited causal evidence. Do not draw causal claims
  from your own cross-sectional associations.
* "We" with the past tense for what was done; present tense for
  interpretation; "should" or "could" for implications.

## Conclusion and final paragraph

* About 85 to 150 words: one take-home claim, at most one or two numbers,
  one action with a named actor, and the key data or research need.
* Abstract Interpretations in top papers (median about 56 words) often name
  a policy, programme, health-system or data implication.
* Do not end on a limitation or on "more research is needed" without saying
  which research.

## Sentence frames

1. "We estimate that [number] ([95% UI]) [deaths] in [place] in [year] were
   attributable to [condition], more than [familiar comparator]."
2. "Our estimate of [number] for [year] is [higher or lower] than the
   [number] reported by [source] for [year]."
3. "Restricted to the [n] [units] covered by both studies, our estimate is
   [number] against their [number]."
4. "Most of this gap comes from [input], which was [value] in [source] and
   [value] here."
5. "The close match with [source] partly reflects shared inputs, so it is not
   independent confirmation."
6. "Our data describe [pattern] but cannot show why; [driver A] and [driver
   B] are plausible explanations that need testing."
7. "Because [related measure] stayed stable while [outcome] fell, [factor]
   is unlikely to explain the change."
8. "If the trend since [year] continues, [population] will reach [value] by
   [year], [far from or close to] the [target]."
9. "In [setting A], where [driver A] dominates, [response A] may help most;
   in [setting B], [response B] fits better."
10. "[Data source] was [problem] in [where], which probably [overstates or
    understates] [estimate]; we [action], and results changed by [amount]."
11. "We cannot tell whether [limitation] pushes [estimate] up or down;
    [specific study or data] would settle this."
12. "Because we included only [scope], our total is a lower bound."
13. "[Outcome] is now [rank or size] in [population]; [named actor] should
    [action], and [data system] needs [improvement] to track progress."

## Weaknesses to avoid

* A short Discussion that mostly restates Results.
* No limitations, or generic ones with no direction, size or remedy.
* Heavy, even hedging that blurs the main message.
* "Consistent with previous studies" with no numbers or reasons.
* "More research is needed" without saying which.
* Policy advice not tied to a finding, or causal advice from descriptive
  data.
* A long Conclusion that repeats results.

## Checklist

* [ ] First paragraph states the main result with a number and its interval
* [ ] Each key finding has a driver, labelled by strength of evidence
* [ ] Your number sits beside at least one named earlier estimate
* [ ] Differences explained by data, definition, method or counterfactual
* [ ] Agreement checked for shared inputs
* [ ] Each implication links to a finding and a named actor
* [ ] Targets tested against observed trends
* [ ] Strengths concrete and brief
* [ ] Each limitation: source, direction, size if possible, and what was done
* [ ] One hedge per explanation; causal verbs only where earned
* [ ] Final paragraph: one take-home claim and one call to action
* [ ] Does not end on a limitation
