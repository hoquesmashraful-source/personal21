# Introduction

## What the corpus shows

* Median 622 words (IQR 493 to 809) in 5 paragraphs.
* 88% state the burden with numbers; 40% cite global targets (SDGs,
  Millennium Development Goals, WHO strategies); 55% describe an evidence
  gap; 65% end with an explicit aim; 18% use a theory or conceptual
  framework.
* In 40 exemplars, top-journal introductions were shorter (about 510 against
  670 words) and reached the gap by paragraph 2 or 3. They usually added a
  sentence on why these data can fill the gap now. All 20 ended with a
  first-person aim naming the data and scale; 9 of 20 others never used "we".

## Moves, in order

1. **Burden or importance** (one paragraph, two to five sentences). The
   problem with no more than three key statistics, each cited; a global or
   national target if relevant.
2. **What is known** (one or two paragraphs). Evidence and mechanisms;
   a framework if one guides the analysis. Synthesise; do not summarise
   study by study.
3. **The gap** (two to five sentences). What earlier work did, why that falls
   short, and why it matters. Strong gap types: no comparable multi-country
   evidence; a neglected dimension (quality rather than access); a
   measurement problem; an update with better data; an open policy debate.
4. **The opportunity** (one or two sentences). Why these data or methods can
   fill the gap now (for example, a new survey module, linkable facility
   data, repeated rounds).
5. **Aim** (final paragraph, one to three sentences). First person, naming
   the data, population, setting and years. Numbered sub-aims or a
   hypothesis where useful.

## Sentence frames

Use `[ref]` placeholders for citations not supplied by the user.

1. "In [year], an estimated [number] [people] [had outcome], [share] of them
   in [region] [ref]."
2. "[Target, such as SDG target 3.1] commits countries to [goal] by [year],
   which requires knowing [missing evidence]."
3. "[Exposure] has been linked to [outcome] in [settings] [refs], probably
   through [mechanism]."
4. "However, these studies [covered single countries, used different
   definitions or relied on facility data], so their results cannot be
   compared or generalised."
5. "Whether [exposure] matters beyond [known factor] has not been examined
   across [scale]."
6. "[Survey] now includes [module or measure] in [N] countries, which allows
   a standardised comparison."
7. "Without this evidence, [programme managers] cannot decide [specific
   choice]."
8. "We aimed to [estimate or quantify] [outcome] in [N] countries and to
   examine whether [question]."
9. "We hypothesised that [direction], because [mechanism]."

## Weaknesses to avoid

* Burden followed directly by the objective, with no gap.
* Burden presented as the reason for the study.
* Vague gaps ("paucity of literature", "few studies") without saying what
  is missing and why it matters.
* Paragraphs stepping from global to regional to national statistics.
* Textbook definitions of standard indicators.
* Study-by-study literature summaries.
* Soft aims ("shed light on", "attempts to", "contribute to the growing
  literature").
* Bare novelty claims. Use "to our knowledge" only when the user has checked
  the literature; otherwise describe what the study adds.
* Sentences copied from the abstract.

## Style

* Present tense for current facts; past tense for specific earlier studies.
* Cite each statistic where it appears.
* Name surveys by country and year ("the 2016 Nepal DHS"); India's DHS is
  the National Family Health Survey.
* "Associated with" in the aim; keep "effect" for causal designs.

## Checklist

* [ ] Four or five paragraphs, roughly 400 to 650 words (or journal limit)
* [ ] Burden in one paragraph with no more than three cited statistics
* [ ] Gap says what earlier studies did and why that is not enough
* [ ] A sentence on why these data can fill the gap
* [ ] Final paragraph states the aim with data, setting and years
* [ ] Novelty claims qualified and verified
